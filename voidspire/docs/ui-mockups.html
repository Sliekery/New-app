Ten ways to show the dice

The prototype currently draws its dice as cards in a fan, because that is the furniture the engine already had. It works, and it is the one thing in the design that still says "deckbuilder" out loud — a die dressed as a card is a costume, not a shape.

Every mock below shows the same turn, so the difference you are looking at is presentation and nothing else.

ORGANISED AS A HAND

Things you hold and choose between. Familiar, thumb-friendly, and inherently card-shaped — which is the tension, since these are dice.

THE FAN — WHAT WE HAVE

Dice and protocols as cards in an arc. Everything the engine already knows how to draw.

READS
Instantly. Everyone knows a hand.
COSTS
It is a lie about the object. Nothing here rolls.

THE RACK

Dice seated as cartridges in a belt; protocols clip on beside them. Rolls are loose rounds you spend.

READS
Ammunition. Fits "Arsenal" better than anything else here.
COSTS
Face tables have nowhere to live — needs a second screen.

THUMB ARC

Everything on one swept arc in the corner. Wall is a ring gauge, rolls are three pips.

READS
Best one-handed play of the ten. Nothing is ever occluded.
COSTS
Only fits ~6 things. The rack cannot grow.

ORGANISED AS THE DIE'S GEOMETRY

The face table is the interface. These make where-you-cut visible during the fight rather than on a separate screen — which is the one thing the current UI cannot do at all.

THE RIBBON

Both dice unrolled into strips. Bands are a coloured underlay; cut faces glow; a marker shows where you landed.

READS
Your build, during the fight. The only mock that shows density.
COSTS
Wide. Eats the bottom third and fights the enemy art.

THE ROTOR

A marker lives on the ring. You roll how far it travels, and everything it crosses fires.

READS
You always know where you start, so density becomes strategy.
COSTS
A rules change, not a reskin. Biggest build on this page.

THE WHEEL

The face table as a wheel, wedged by band. The centre reads out what you landed on.

READS
Bands become spatial — you feel the odds instead of learning them.
COSTS
Circles are expensive on screen. Two of them is most of a phone.

ORGANISED AS A MACHINE

The fiction does the work. You are not holding a hand, you are operating a weapon — which is what the class is actually called.

GUN SCHEMATIC

Barrel is the d20, its cut faces marked along the bore. Guard is a plate. Protocols clip to a rail.

READS
Strongest fiction of the ten. Engraving becomes gunsmithing, literally.
COSTS
A 20-segment bore is small. Cramped below tablet size.

THE CONSOLE

Gauges, a pressure bar and physical toggles. Every value has a dial instead of a number.

READS
State at a glance. Wall as pressure is the best metaphor here.
COSTS
Cold. You are reading instruments, not making a decision.

THE BENCH

Top-down. Real objects on a work surface, each with its face table written out beside it like a spec sheet.

READS
Calm and complete. Nothing hidden behind a second screen.
COSTS
Static. It looks like an inventory, not a fight.

THE TRAY

Actual dice in a felt tray. Wall stacks as chips, protocols are tokens, rolls are three markers.

READS
Tactile. The only one where throwing feels like throwing.
COSTS
A d20 showing one face hides the nineteen that matter.

WHAT I'D ACTUALLY DO

The ten sort into one real question: does the face table live in the fight, or on a separate screen? Right now it lives on a separate screen, and that is why the dice feel like cards — you build a thing you never see while using it.

  1. THE RIBBON for the build. It is the only mock where you can see density — the thing every engraving decision is actually about — without leaving combat. Bands as a coloured underlay solve the other quiet problem: nobody currently knows what a 14 is worth without counting.
  2. GUN SCHEMATIC for the identity. It costs nothing mechanically and it makes engraving read as gunsmithing, which is what the class has always been about. The ribbon can be the bore.
  3. THE ROTOR if you want to change the game rather than the picture. It is the only one here that alters what a roll means, and it is the one that would make a dense die feel earned.

The combination I'd build: a schematic frame, with each die's faces drawn as a ribbon along it, and the wall as a pressure bar borrowed from the console. Protocols stay clipped to a rail. Nothing in the fight is a card any more.
